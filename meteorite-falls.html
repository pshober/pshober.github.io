After a bright fireball, meteorites fall the last few kilometres in the dark, and weather radars often see them on the way down. These are the falls whose radar signatures were found by MetDetect, my automated search, or identified by hand, with maps of where the stones most likely landed.

How it works

  1. A fireball breaks up

    Most of a meteoroid burns up. What survives slows down within seconds and stops glowing 15–40 km up. From there the stones fall in the dark for several minutes, drifting with the wind: the dark flight.

  2. Weather radars see the stones

    NEXRAD radars scan the sky every few minutes. A shower of falling stones and dust can show up as faint, short-lived echoes that sink from one scan to the next, sometimes on several radars at once.

  3. MetDetect searches automatically

    Given when and roughly where a fireball happened, MetDetect searches the weather-radar data from around that time for the echo of falling debris and outlines the radar cells it covers. Every search returns an answer: it locates the debris of a known fall, it does not decide whether one happened. Each fall is scored by a model trained without it.

  4. A person verifies it

    Automatic detections stay awaiting verification until someone has checked the radar data by hand. The human-verified falls here were identified by Marc Fries; MetDetect is graded against his identifications.

  5. Dark flight gives a search map

    Each radar cell is flown down through the winds of that day, which shows where stones of each mass most likely landed. Recovered meteorites are drawn on the maps afterwards, as a check.
